## Restoration emergencies common in Chicago

Chicago's weather swings hard between extremes, and both ends cause damage. Winter brings deep freeze cycles that crack supply lines run through uninsulated exterior walls or crawlspaces, and pipes that burst overnight are often discovered as ceiling stains or warped hardwood by morning. Spring and summer bring heavy thunderstorm systems off Lake Michigan that overwhelm aging combined sewer systems, pushing water back through floor drains and into basements across the city. The housing stock compounds both problems: a large share of Chicago's homes were built before mid-century, with masonry foundations and basements that were never fully waterproofed to modern standards, so once water gets in, it tends to stay until it's mechanically removed and the space is dried per the IICRC S500 drying standard.

## Services we provide in Chicago

Water damage restoration is the most frequent call, and in Chicago it usually means basement flooding from sewer backup or foundation seepage, or upper-floor leaks from frozen and burst supply lines. We extract standing water, pull wet flooring and drywall where needed, and set drying equipment to bring moisture content back down before mold has a chance to establish.

Fire and smoke damage cleanup addresses both the structural loss and the lingering odor that soaks into plaster, trim, and fabric. Chicago's older buildings often have plaster walls and original woodwork, which hold smoke odor differently than modern drywall and require different cleaning agents and sealing steps.

Storm damage response covers wind, hail, and flood-related losses, including roof and window damage from the severe thunderstorm lines that move through the area and the basement flooding that follows heavy rain events.

Mold remediation follows almost any water event that goes undetected for more than a day or two, which is common in finished basements and behind plaster walls where moisture isn't visible until it's already spread.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Chicago

Much of Chicago's housing was built from the late 1800s through the mid-1900s, with brick and masonry construction, full basements, and plumbing that has often been updated piecemeal over the decades, meaning a single building can have galvanized steel, copper, and PEX all in the same system. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside and fails without warning, while older buildings may still carry lead service lines or asbestos-containing materials in flooring and pipe insulation, both of which affect how a restoration scope is planned and who needs to be involved before demolition starts.

The city's flat terrain and clay-heavy soil drain slowly, which is part of why basement seepage and sewer backup are recurring problems after heavy rain. Structural repairs and rebuild work typically require a permit through the City of Chicago's Department of Buildings, and older masonry buildings in particular often need a closer look at party walls and foundation conditions before repair work begins.
