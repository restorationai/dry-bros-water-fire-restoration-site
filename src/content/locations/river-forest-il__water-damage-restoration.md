River Forest sits along the Des Plaines River on Chicago's near west side, and that geography shapes how water damage plays out here. Basements in this part of Cook County sit close to the water table, and many of the village's homes date to the early-to-mid 1900s, with fieldstone or brick foundations that were never built to modern waterproofing standards. When those foundations take on water, whether from a spring thaw, a sump failure, or a supply line that lets go behind a plaster wall, the moisture doesn't just sit on the surface. It works into joists, subfloor, and masonry in ways that aren't always obvious until the smell shows up weeks later.

## Why River Forest Properties See Water Damage Issues

The housing stock is the first factor. Older River Forest homes often still have original cast iron or galvanized plumbing, and both materials corrode from the inside out over decades, which means a pinhole leak can go undetected inside a wall cavity for a long time before it surfaces as a stain or a soft spot in the floor. The second factor is drainage. Like much of inner-ring Chicago suburbs, parts of River Forest rely on combined or aging storm and sanitary infrastructure, and a heavy spring or summer downpour can push water back up through floor drains faster than a sump pump can keep pace. Add in mature trees with root systems that have had seventy or eighty years to find their way into clay sewer laterals, and you've got a village where water intrusion tends to come from below as often as it comes from a failed appliance or a frozen pipe.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in River Forest

The first call is about stopping the source and getting water off the floor, whether that's a standing puddle in a finished basement rec room or water tracking under original hardwood in a 1920s bungalow. From there we extract with truck-mounted or portable units depending on access, pull baseboards and, where the wall cavity is wet, open it enough to get air moving without taking out more plaster than necessary. Drying is calibrated to the material: plaster and old-growth lumber hold moisture longer than modern drywall and dimensional lumber, so we track readings daily with moisture meters rather than guessing at a drying timeline. Once materials read dry per IICRC S500 guidance, we document everything for your claim before any rebuild starts.

## Equipment & Methods We Use for Water Damage

Structural drying in a village like River Forest usually means a mix of low-grain refrigerant dehumidifiers for finished basements and higher-capacity desiccant units when we're drying out masonry foundations, since stone and old brick release moisture slowly and need sustained airflow over days, not hours. Air movers get positioned to push air across wet wall cavities and under flooring rather than just blowing across a room, and we use thermal imaging early on to find wet pockets in plaster and subfloor that a moisture meter alone might miss on a first pass.

## Local note

A lot of River Forest basements have finished space built directly against the original fieldstone foundation wall, often with furring strips and drywall added decades after the house was built. When that wall takes on water, the drywall can look fine while the furring strips and the stone behind it stay damp for days, which is why we always check behind finished basement walls in older River Forest homes rather than relying on a surface-dry reading.
