## Why Riverside Floods the Way It Does

Riverside was laid out by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux in the 1870s, with curving streets following the natural contours of the Des Plaines River rather than a flat grid. That design is beautiful and historic, but it also means a lot of the village's housing stock sits close to the river's floodplain on lots that were graded for drainage patterns from a century and a half ago, not for the storm volumes we see now. Many of these homes still have original rubble-stone or brick foundations with limited or no modern waterproofing, so when the river rises or storm sewers back up, water finds its way through old mortar joints and foundation cracks rather than pooling neatly on top of a slab. Mature parkway trees, another Olmsted signature, add another wrinkle: root systems can shift foundation stone over decades and create new entry points for groundwater that weren't there when the house was built.

## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Riverside

Flood cleanup in a historic Riverside home starts with identifying what the water actually touched, because stone foundations, plaster walls, and original hardwood subfloors all hold and release moisture differently than modern materials. We pull standing water first, then use moisture meters to map how far it's traveled up masonry and under flooring, since stone can wick water several feet above the visible waterline. Drying is handled per the IICRC S500 standard for water damage, with air movers and dehumidifiers positioned to work with the home's existing ventilation rather than against it, since older Riverside houses often have limited duct runs or no central HVAC in the basement at all. Where plaster and lath are involved, drying takes longer than drywall, and that timeline gets built into the plan up front instead of being a surprise on day three.

## Riverside Insurance & Floodplain Coordination

A meaningful share of Riverside properties sit in or near a FEMA-designated floodplain given the village's riverside location, which means flood damage is often covered under a separate NFIP flood policy rather than a standard homeowners policy. We document the loss with photos and moisture readings formatted for either type of claim and work directly with adjusters so the paperwork doesn't become a second project on top of the cleanup. Because Riverside is a designated National Historic Landmark district, exterior repairs and some structural work may also need to go through the village's historic preservation review in addition to standard Cook County permitting, and we flag that early so it doesn't stall the restoration timeline.

One thing worth knowing if you're new to the area: the Des Plaines River's crest in Riverside typically lags behind upstream rainfall by a day or more, so a storm that's already cleared out can still mean rising water in low-lying yards and basements well after the sky is blue again. Homeowners who only check for flooding during or right after a storm sometimes miss the actual high-water point entirely.
