## Why does certification matter for fire and smoke restoration?

Certification means the technicians working in your home were trained to a documented industry standard for fire and smoke restoration, not just taught on the job by whoever was available that week. The [ANSI/IICRC S700 Standard for Professional Fire and Smoke Damage Restoration](https://iicrc.org/s700/) lays out how soot type, surface porosity, and odor source should be assessed and treated, which matters because wet-smoke residue from a kitchen fire behaves very differently from dry-smoke residue from a structure fire.

Ask any company you're considering a direct question: "Do your technicians hold Fire and Smoke Restoration Technician (FSRT) training?" A company that can answer specifically, rather than vaguely gesturing at "certified technicians," is one that treats the standard as a working document, not a marketing line. Don't take "we're certified" at face value; ask which credential, held by whom.

## What should you ask about insurance billing and contents pack-out?

Ask whether the company documents damage in a format your adjuster can actually use, and whether they can pack out and store salvageable belongings separately from the active worksite. Soot-damaged furniture, electronics, and clothing often need off-site cleaning in an ozone chamber or ultrasonic bath, not treatment in a smoke-filled house.

A contents restoration line item matters because it protects items that would otherwise get thrown away out of convenience. Ask to see how a company photographs and inventories contents before they're moved. That inventory is also the backbone of a smoother insurance claim, since the [Insurance Information Institute notes that documentation quality is one of the biggest factors in how quickly a claim settles](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster).
